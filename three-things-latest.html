The FCA Wants Savers in Property Funds to Wait 90 Days for Their Cash

The FCA proposes that investors in retail funds holding mainly property, infrastructure and other hard-to-sell assets give at least 90 days' notice before taking money out. The rule would cover non-UCITS retail schemes invested mainly in such assets. Existing funds would get two years to comply and would have to give their investors at least a year's warning. The consultation, CP26/35, closes in December.

Why this matters

Banks' wealth platforms, fund managers and advisers that sell these funds lose the promise of daily cash on them. Platforms and model portfolios built around daily dealing have to make room for a three-month wait. In return, managers can hold less idle cash against redemptions, the drag on returns the FCA wants to remove.

Britain Blacklists Crypto Exchanges in Its Latest Russia Package

Britain's Foreign Office designated 38 targets in one package, including 12 tankers it says belong to Russia's shadow fleet, three crypto exchanges and two payment platforms. Two of the designated entities handled payments for A7, a Kremlin-backed network for moving money around sanctions. A7 claimed to have moved more than £68.0bn ($90bn) last year, the government says. A newspaper investigation, from a single source so far, found that A7 listed a British fintech registered with the FCA as one of its payment channels. The firm was not named.

Why this matters

Crypto exchanges and payment platforms now sit on the same sanctions list as tankers, so British payment firms, e-money institutions and the banks that sponsor them carry a wider screening job. If the investigation holds, a firm on the FCA's register sat inside a sanctioned network's payment chain. That makes a sponsor bank's checks on its fintech clients a sanctions exposure in its own right.

3

Revolut Plans American Credit Cards Once It Holds a Bank Charter

Revolut plans to offer credit cards and loans in America once it holds a full national bank charter, its chief executive, Nik Storonsky, said in a television interview. Revolut already has preliminary approval. He said the company would prefer a primary listing in New York if it goes public, and that any listing is at least a year away. The listing preference is not new; the credit plan is.

Why this matters

American card issuers and consumer lenders gain a rival that plans to turn its app users into borrowers, competing for the card balances and loan interest where a consumer bank makes most of its money. For British banks fighting Revolut at home, its lending growth, and probably its listing, would come in America, not London.
