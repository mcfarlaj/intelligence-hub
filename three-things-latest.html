Federal Reserve Proposes Rules for Banks That Issue Stablecoins

The Federal Reserve Board has published two proposals under the GENIUS Act, America's stablecoin law. One would make issuers it supervises back coins fully with permitted reserves, such as short-term Treasury bills, and face standard capital charges. The other sets out how banks it supervises apply to issue one. The FDIC, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Treasury had already published drafts, completing the set before the law takes effect in 2027.

Why this matters

American banks can now start costing their own dollar coins, though no capital ratios were given. On one reading, the text presumes some yield arrangements run through third parties to be prohibited interest. A coin that cannot pay a return by the back door competes less directly with a savings account, and so with any incumbent's deposit franchise.

2

White House Asks OpenAI and Anthropic to Put American Testers First

The White House has asked OpenAI and Anthropic not to give new models to Britain's AI Security Institute until the US government has tested them. A senior administration official said this has been policy for every new frontier model. Anthropic did not provide Claude Mythos 5.1 to the institute, saying it was available only to a set of US organisations. The report rests on one outlet and anonymous sources.

Why this matters

Britain set up the institute to test the most capable models before release. If American testers go first, the assurance UK firms and supervisors draw from it arrives later and on Washington's terms.

3

Bank of England's Breeden Edges Towards a Rate Rise

Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden said it was becoming 'increasingly appropriate' for Bank Rate to respond to rising inflation risks. She was not ready to vote for a rise this month; her focus is whether the first move comes at the next decision. Bank Rate stands at 3.75 per cent, and markets price about a percentage point of rises over the coming year. The ten-year gilt yield touched 5.39 per cent during trading, its highest since 2007.

Why this matters

A rise at the next decision is now a live option, which lifts fixed mortgage pricing and bank funding costs together. The gilt market has already moved, so that repricing has started before any vote.
