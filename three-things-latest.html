Fiserv Puts a State Bank's Stablecoin Inside Community Banks' Software

Fiserv supplies the core systems on which many American banks and credit unions run their accounts, and its digital asset platform is now live with bank clients. Its first live use is Roughrider Coin, a dollar stablecoin from Bank of North Dakota, which the state owns. More than 90 North Dakota banks and credit unions can now use it to move money between themselves through Commercial Center, the online banking console Fiserv already supplies to them. VersaBank issues the coin, Fireblocks supplies the tokenisation software and transactions run on Solana. Fiserv disclosed no volumes or money figures.

Why this matters

Stablecoins can now reach small banks through the vendors they already pay, so a small lender gets a stablecoin without building anything. If UK core-banking suppliers follow Fiserv, tokenised money will reach lenders that would never have built it themselves.

2

Washington Brands a Kremlin-Linked Payment Network a Criminal Organisation

The US Treasury has designated the A7 Network, a Kremlin-linked cross-border payments group, a significant transnational criminal organisation. Treasury says A7 moved money for Iran's central bank, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps and groups Iran backs. FinCEN, the Treasury's financial-crimes unit, has proposed barring any transfer involving A7's sub-agents, and issued an alert asking banks to spot and report A7-linked payments. Treasury's own count is that sub-agents moved more than £12.9bn ($17bn) over 18 months.

Why this matters

A bank whose correspondents touch A7's sub-agents now faces a named US criminal designation as well as sanctions screening, which raises the cost of a missed payment. The tool is more often aimed at gangs and cartels; aimed at a payments network, it reaches straight into correspondent banking.

3

Draft Prospectus Shows Broadcom Financing Anthropic's Chip Bill

Broadcom has agreed to lend Anthropic up to £31.8bn ($42bn) to pay for computing infrastructure, according to Anthropic's draft listing prospectus, which a news agency has seen. The money comes as convertible notes that could turn into Anthropic shares. The loan could cover about a third of Anthropic's £94.8bn ($125.2bn) five-year commitment to rent TPU computing capacity. The prospectus flags possible conflicts of interest, since Broadcom is both supplier and lender. No public registration statement has been filed.

Why this matters

When chip suppliers lend to their customers, a bank lending to either side is exposed to both: a fall in AI demand would hit the supplier's sales and its loan book at once.
